About Deer Gallery

Deer Gallery began as a coursework archive: eight field photographs, a folder of rejected shots, and a list of things the animals taught us that no guidebook mentioned. It is published here as a study in semantic HTML, CSS layout and accessible design.

Contact: photographs, corrections and species identifications can go to hello@deergallery.example. Identification disputes are welcome. Two of the images on the gallery page are still listed as probable rather than certain.

Conservation: where the work goes

Deer are not usually the species people rally to protect. They are abundant, adaptable, and in many regions overabundant. That makes them an unusual conservation teacher: the problem is rarely the animal, almost always the landscape around it.

Four things this project spends its print income on:

  • Wildlife crossings. Fencing that funnels deer under a road rather than across it, which is where most mortality happens.
  • Woodland edge habitat. Native shrub strips between forest and field, because that boundary layer is where fawns feed.
  • Population monitoring. Counting seasons, not numbers: breeding females and fawn survival tell you more than a headcount.
  • Access for photographers. Funding hides and permission fees at reserves, so people can shoot from a distance instead of walking in.

A red deer stag in a managed park. This kind of population survives because somebody budgets for winter fencing and summer counts.

How to tell if a wildlife photograph was made ethically

Anybody can put a deer in a frame. The honest ones leave evidence in the image.

  • The animal is feeding, resting or moving, not staring at the lens in alarm.
  • Compression from a long lens, not proximity. Backgrounds stacked tight behind the subject mean distance was respected.
  • No visible fence, salt lick, bait pile or feeder in the frame.
  • A fawn photographed with the doe nearby, or not photographed at all.
  • Location kept vague in the caption, so the site does not become a destination.
